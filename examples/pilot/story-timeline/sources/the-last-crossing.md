# The Last Crossing

## S01 — Chapter 1: The clock above the door

At eight that evening, the river looked like a strip of unpolished metal. Rain trembled on the ferry office windows. Mara set two cups beside the stove, although she had been drinking alone since the winter timetable began. Across the water, a lamp burned in the locked workshop where her brother once repaired boats. Its reflection broke whenever the current carried a branch beneath the footbridge.

The office smelled of damp paper and orange peel. Someone had left a newspaper under the ticket hatch, folded to an advertisement for a house with a south-facing garden. Mara smoothed the paper without reading the price. She knew the house: a red roof above the road, a gate that caught on the pavement. From its garden, perhaps, the river would seem no wider than a road.

Her brother's last postcard lay beneath the timetable. He had written about the heat in a town where she had never been, and drawn a square to show the window of his room. There was nothing about coming home. She turned the postcard over, then put it back beneath the timetable, leaving the little square visible.

For the first part of the evening she had stayed inside, listening to the stove settle. Now she lifted the canvas bag from its peg and opened it. There was nothing in it, not even the receipt she usually forgot in the bottom. She took the workshop key from the drawer and put on her coat.

The office clock showed twenty minutes past eight when Mara stepped out. It was 20:20. The minute hand settled against its mark as the door swung shut behind her. She took the narrow footbridge toward the workshop, carrying the empty bag.

## S02 — Chapter 2: The light across the water

Halfway across, Mara stopped to free a strand of wool caught on a nail. The sleeve had belonged to her mother before it belonged to her; one careless pull would leave a ladder of loose stitches. She bent close, lifted the strand with the end of the key, and watched a drop fall from her cuff into the river. It vanished without a sound she could distinguish from the water below.

The river sounded heavier beneath the bridge than it did from the office. She wondered whether the workshop lamp had been left on deliberately, then disliked herself for turning a light into a message. There were ordinary reasons for lights. Her brother used to leave this one burning while he ate his supper, because the switch was behind a stack of timber and he could never be bothered to reach it twice.

At the workshop she tried her old key. It did not turn. She drew it out, examined the worn teeth, and tried again more gently. The door remained shut. A line of fresh paint ran around the lock; a flake came away beneath her nail. She rubbed the flake between her fingers until it disappeared.

Through the window she could see a chair, a coil of rope and a white envelope on the bench. She could not read the writing. The glass reflected her face whenever she leaned closer, laying her eyes over the place where the address should have been. Beyond the bench, a wooden hull rested on supports with its ribs exposed.

She stayed outside, with rain running from her sleeves into her hands. The bag brushed her knee each time the wind moved it. Eventually she turned back without the envelope, leaving the lamp and the locked door behind her.

## S03 — Chapter 3: Two cups

Mara re-entered the ferry office at 20:55 that same evening. The same clock stood above the door; it had continued running correctly. The bag was still empty. She had made no other departure from the office that evening, and this was her return from the walk across the footbridge.

One cup had developed a pale skin. She carried both cups to the sink before taking off her coat. The water in the cold tap rattled through the pipes and struck the enamel basin hard enough to splash her sleeve. She rinsed the cups and set them upside down on the draining board, close together but not touching.

A bus passed without stopping. Its windows briefly laid squares of yellow light across the floor. Mara watched those squares move away and thought of the envelope remaining dry on the bench. A passenger's face had appeared for an instant behind the last window; by the time she looked toward the road, only the red rear lights remained.

She left her key on the desk, beside the folded winter timetable. Water from the coat made a small dark patch beneath its peg. The stove had begun to lose its warmth. She pushed a piece of wood into the grate and waited while a narrow flame found the split edge.

On the wall was a photograph of the old ferry, taken before the footbridge existed. Her brother stood at the edge of the picture, holding a rope. The photographer had cut off his left hand. Mara had looked at that photograph for years without noticing the missing hand; now the crop seemed impossible to overlook.

## S04 — Chapter 4: The watchman's news

Elias, the night watchman, arrived later. He had been in the storeroom during Mara's absence. He brought a smell of cardboard and the cold air trapped between stacked boxes. A strip of brown tape clung to his shoe. He noticed it only when Mara looked down, and scraped it against the leg of the desk.

Looking at her wet coat, he said, “You came back at 20:10. I remember because the news had just begun.”

“From the workshop?” he added, nodding toward the opposite bank. “That walk you took this evening. Ten past eight, when you came back over the bridge.”

Mara looked up at the clock. She neither agreed nor corrected him. Elias filled the kettle and began describing a road closure. A lorry had broken down near the junction; the buses would have to turn behind the school. He traced the detour with one finger on the newspaper, passing through the advertised house and its south-facing garden.

The kettle began to make a low ticking sound. Elias took one of the rinsed cups and dried it with a towel. He asked whether the workshop would open again before spring, but his eyes were on the route he had drawn. Mara turned the newspaper so that it would no longer overhang the edge of the desk.

Outside, a gust shook the ticket hatch. Elias tested its catch, then pulled the office door firmly into its frame. The clock kept moving above him. When the kettle boiled, he poured the water too quickly and carried his cup to the window with a saucer beneath it.

## S05 — Chapter 5: The key on the sill

When Elias left, Mara moved the key from the desk to the windowsill. She did not throw it away. Nor did she go back across the bridge. The workshop lamp was still visible, a small yellow rectangle suspended above the black water.

She wiped the ring the saucer had left on the desk. Beneath it, the newspaper had softened. The house with the south-facing garden now had a pale circle across its roof. She folded the paper along its old crease and pushed it under the ticket hatch, where it had been before she went out.

The rain had thinned enough for separate drops to sound on the metal awning. Across the river, the footbridge boards shone in a row. The empty bag hung again from its peg. Its mouth had fallen open, showing the seam at the bottom and a loose thread caught in the lining.

Mara switched off the office light and stood for a moment beside the dark window. From the road, anyone looking in would have seen the kettle, the timetable and the outline of a woman waiting. The key lay within reach of her hand. Nothing more was said about the time, and what she intended to do with the key remained unsaid.
